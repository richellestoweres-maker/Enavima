Understanding the disease

Congenital cytomegalovirus, explained

CMV is one of the most common viruses in the world and almost never a problem for a healthy adult. When it crosses the placenta, it becomes something else entirely.

What CMV is

Cytomegalovirus is a member of the herpesvirus family, alongside herpes simplex virus types 1 and 2, varicella-zoster virus and Epstein-Barr virus. Like the rest of that family, it stays in the body for life after the first infection, usually without causing any symptoms.

It is extraordinarily common. Nearly one in three children in the United States has been infected with CMV by age five, and more than half of adults have been infected by age 40. Most never know it.

How it spreads

CMV is transmitted through body fluids, principally saliva and urine, and particularly from young children, who can shed the virus for months after infection. It also spreads through sexual contact, breast milk, transplanted organs and blood transfusions, and from a pregnant woman to her developing baby.

That last route is the reason this page exists.

What congenital CMV is

When CMV passes from a pregnant woman to the fetus, the result is congenital CMV infection, or cCMV. About 1 in 200 babies in the United States is born with it, making it the most common infectious cause of birth defects in the country.

The central difficulty

Roughly 90% of babies born with congenital CMV have no signs at birth. They are discharged as healthy newborns. Some of them will go on to develop hearing loss or developmental problems months or years later, long after the test that could have named the cause was still valid.

Babies with signs at birth

About 10% of infected infants show signs at birth. These can include petechiae, jaundice, microcephaly, growth restriction, hepatosplenomegaly, seizures and retinitis. Of the babies who do have signs at birth, roughly 40 to 60% will have long-term health problems, such as hearing loss, developmental delays, vision problems, microcephaly, coordination difficulties or seizures.

Babies without signs at birth

Most infants with congenital CMV stay healthy. But a portion develop delayed-onset hearing loss that a newborn hearing screen cannot detect, because at the time of the screen their hearing is normal.

Taken across both groups, about 1 in 5 babies born with congenital CMV will have a birth defect or other long-term health problem, and hearing loss is the most common of them.

How it is diagnosed, and why timing decides everything

Congenital CMV is diagnosed by detecting the virus itself, not the body’s response to it. Antibody testing cannot diagnose congenital CMV. CDC guidance points to saliva by PCR as the preferred specimen, with confirmation on urine.

The critical constraint is when. Testing must be done within two to three weeks of birth. In CDC’s words: if testing is done after three weeks, it is not possible to distinguish between congenital CMV infection and infection acquired after birth.

This is not a technicality. Whether an infection was congenital is what determines whether antiviral treatment is considered and whether the child is enrolled in structured audiologic and developmental follow-up. After day 21, a positive CMV test no longer answers that question.

Treatment

Antiviral medications, such as ganciclovir or valganciclovir, may improve hearing and developmental outcomes in newborns with signs of congenital CMV. Evidence remains more limited for treating isolated hearing loss. Treatment decisions belong to the treating clinician, weighed against the drugs’ side effects and the individual infant’s presentation.

Regardless of whether antivirals are used, infants diagnosed with cCMV need regular hearing and vision testing. Early detection of hearing loss is what protects communication and language development.

Prevention during pregnancy

Because young children are the most common source, the practical prevention advice centers on contact with toddler saliva and urine:

  • Wash your hands with soap and water after changing diapers, wiping a nose, or helping a child use the toilet.
  • Do not share food, utensils, cups, straws or pacifiers with young children.
  • Avoid contact with a young child’s saliva when kissing. A kiss on the forehead rather than the lips.
  • Clean toys, countertops and other surfaces that come into contact with saliva or urine.

These steps are simple, and most pregnant women have never been told them, which is a large part of why cCMV remains as common as it is.

A note on what this page is

This is general education, not medical advice for you or your baby. If you are pregnant, or your newborn is within the first three weeks of life and you have concerns about CMV, talk to your obstetrician, midwife or pediatrician directly. The timing matters too much to wait.
