For parents & expecting families

If you are pregnant, or your baby was just born

There are two things worth knowing about CMV. One takes about a minute to learn and can lower your risk during pregnancy. The other has a deadline — roughly three weeks after your baby is born.

During pregnancy

CMV is a common virus. Most adults who catch it never feel sick. But a first infection during pregnancy can pass to your baby, and about 1 in 3 pregnant women who become infected will pass it on.

The most common source is not another adult — it is toddler saliva and urine. If you have a young child at home, or you work in childcare, these habits matter most:

  • Wash your hands with soap and water after changing a diaper, wiping a nose, or helping a child use the toilet.
  • Do not share food, drinks or utensils with young children — no finishing their cup, no shared straws, no cleaning a pacifier in your mouth.
  • Kiss on the forehead or cheek rather than on the lips, and avoid contact with saliva.
  • Clean toys and surfaces that have had saliva or urine on them.

None of this is about being fearful or distant with your child. It is a handful of habits, and most pregnant women are never told about them at all.

In the first three weeks after birth

Congenital CMV can only be confirmed with a test done in roughly the first 21 days of life. After that, a positive test cannot tell anyone whether your baby was born with the infection or picked it up afterward — and that distinction is what determines whether treatment and follow-up are offered.

The newborn hearing screen is not a CMV test

They are two different things. Your baby can pass the hearing screen and still have congenital CMV. Hearing loss from cCMV often begins months or years later — well past the window when a test could have explained it.

If your state does not screen every newborn, testing generally happens only if your baby fails the hearing screen or has other signs. You can ask for it anyway.

Bring this with you

What to ask your provider

You do not need to know the science. These four questions cover it.

1. “Does our hospital test newborns for congenital CMV?”

Some states require it for every baby, some only after a failed hearing screen, and some not at all.

2. “Can my baby be tested for CMV before we are discharged?”

A saliva or urine sample is the usual approach. Doing it before you go home is the simplest way not to miss the window.

3. “If we are already home, how quickly can this be done?”

Count from your baby’s date of birth. Day 21 is the deadline, not a guideline.

4. “If the test is positive, who follows my baby — and how often?”

A positive result should lead to a plan: audiology, ophthalmology, developmental follow-up, and a conversation about antiviral treatment.

If the test comes back positive

A diagnosis is not a prognosis

Most babies born with congenital CMV grow up without any related health problem. About 1 in 5 will have a birth defect or long-term issue — which means four out of five will not. A positive result does not tell you which group your child is in. It tells you that someone will be watching closely enough to catch it early if something develops.

What usually follows a positive test:

  • Confirmation. A saliva result is typically confirmed on urine.
  • A clinical evaluation looking for signs at birth — growth, head size, liver and spleen, blood counts, and often a hearing and eye exam.
  • A treatment conversation. Antiviral medication may be considered for babies with signs at birth; it has real side effects and is not right for every baby.
  • Ongoing hearing and vision checks through early childhood, because hearing loss can start later.

Early detection of hearing loss is the single biggest lever here. Children whose hearing loss is found early and supported early do markedly better with language and communication.

You are not the first family to get this news

The National CMV Foundation maintains parent resources and connects families who have been through it. Your pediatrician can also refer you to a pediatric infectious disease specialist.

Common questions

Questions parents actually ask

Did I do something wrong?

No. CMV is one of the most common viruses there is, most people who carry it never had a symptom, and the prevention advice is not something most women are ever given. Congenital CMV is a public health information failure far more than it is a parenting one.

Can I still breastfeed if I have CMV?

CMV can pass through breast milk, but for a full-term baby the benefits of breastfeeding are generally considered to outweigh that risk. For premature or very low birth weight infants the calculus can differ. This is a conversation to have with your pediatrician about your specific baby.

My baby is already older than three weeks. What now?

A CMV test can still be run, but it can no longer establish that the infection was congenital. In some cases a stored newborn dried blood spot can be tested retrospectively, though this method misses some true cases. Talk to your pediatrician — and if hearing or development is the concern, pursue that evaluation on its own merits regardless of CMV status.

Is there a vaccine?

Not at present. Several CMV vaccine candidates have been studied and development continues, but none is currently approved and available. Prevention today means the hygiene steps above; detection means testing inside the newborn window.

Will insurance cover the test?

It varies by state, plan and whether your state mandates screening. In states with universal newborn screening it is part of the state program. Ask your hospital’s billing office before discharge rather than after.
